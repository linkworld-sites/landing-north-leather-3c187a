A belt gets more daily friction than almost anything else you wear — buckled and unbuckled every morning, flexed at the same point all day, cinched against a buckle that either ages with it or wears through it. Most belts are built from layered, coated scraps and don't survive that friction for long. Here's what separates a belt made for everyday wear for years from one that's cracking within a season.

## One strip of full-grain hide, not layered scraps

Cheaper belts are usually built from bonded or corrected leather — offcuts pressed together and coated to look uniform, with a printed grain standing in for the real thing. Once that coating wears through at the crease, there's nothing underneath but scrap. The Belt is cut as a single strip of full-grain hide instead: the top layer of the leather, left as it grew, with nothing sanded off and nothing coated over. There's no lining inside it to wear through, and nothing hidden underneath to give out once the surface does. What you buy is what ages.

## Why "no lining" matters more on a belt than almost anywhere else

A belt bends at the same point on the same day, day after day. Any lined or layered construction eventually delaminates at exactly that fold — the surface cracks or peels away from the layer underneath, and the belt is finished. Because the Belt is a single strip of hide all the way through, there's no seam between layers to fail. It simply softens and creases at the fold the way a solid piece of leather does, rather than splitting apart.

## Raw brass, not plated hardware

The buckle takes the same daily handling as the strap. Plated hardware sheds its finish within a year of that friction, going dull and grey underneath. The Belt's buckle is raw, unlacquered brass, so it tarnishes and darkens instead — ageing alongside the leather rather than shedding a coating it never really had.
