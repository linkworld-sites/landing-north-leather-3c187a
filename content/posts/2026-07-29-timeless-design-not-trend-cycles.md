Every season, somewhere, a new bag shape gets declared essential and last season's gets quietly retired. It's a good business model if the goal is to sell the same person four bags a year. It's a bad way to design something meant to be carried for a decade, because a shape built to look current in 2026 is, by design, built to look dated in 2027.

We designed the Weekender, the Field Tote, and the Belt once. We haven't touched the silhouettes since, and we don't plan to.

## Trend and durability pull in opposite directions

A design tied to a trend has to keep changing to stay relevant — that's the entire mechanism. A design built to last has to do the opposite: hold a shape simple and proportionate enough that it doesn't read as "this year's" anything. Clean lines, honest materials, hardware that does a job rather than makes a statement. Nothing about it dates, because nothing about it was chasing a moment to begin with.

This is also why we don't run limited colorways or capsule collaborations. Full-grain, vegetable-tanned hide only comes in the range of tones the tanning process actually produces — chestnut, honey, the deep cognac it turns into with age. We're not being precious about it. There's no version of "this season's shade" that doesn't involve corrected, artificially dyed leather, which brings us back to the coating problem: paint over the grain and you've traded the thing that makes it last for the thing that makes it match a mood board.

## What "timeless" actually costs

Timeless design is often talked about like it's free — just don't chase trends, and you're done. In practice it's the harder design brief. A trend gives you a shortcut: make it look like what's currently popular and people will recognize it as current. Without that shortcut, a shape has to earn its own proportions, and there's no hiding a weak line behind this season's hardware finish.

It's slower to design. It's also the only approach where the bag you buy today still looks considered a decade from now, instead of looking like a photograph of a particular year.

## The trade we're making

We'd rather sell one bag that a person uses for ten years than four that get replaced every two or three. That's a smaller number on a spreadsheet and, we think, the only version of this business worth running.
