## Full-grain versus corrected-grain: what you're actually buying

"Leather" on a tag doesn't tell you much. Full-grain is the top layer of the hide, left exactly as it grew — every pore, wrinkle, and natural mark still in it, nothing sanded away and nothing coated over to fake a uniform finish. It's the strongest, densest part of the hide, and it's the only grade that ages: the surface darkens and softens under handling instead of wearing through.

Corrected-grain is what's underneath. The natural surface gets sanded off to erase imperfections, then a printed, artificial grain and a plastic-like finish get stamped on top to make it look uniform again. That coating is doing all the work — once it cracks or peels, which it does within a season or two of daily use, there's nothing left but sanded-down scrap. It can't patina, because there's no real surface left to patina. It can only wear out.

## How a vegetable-tanned honey tote patinas with daily use

Vegetable-tanned leather starts pale, almost blonde, and honey-colored. Left alone it would darken slowly just from light exposure — but a tote isn't left alone. It's handled every day, and that handling is what actually drives the change. Natural oils from your hands, friction against your side or the crook of your arm, sun on the commute, the flex of being opened and closed: all of it works the surface, and vegetable-tanned leather responds by deepening in color and softening in hand.

On the Field Tote specifically, that shows up first at the strap, where it moves with your shoulder and softens fastest, and at the corners and base, where daily contact is heaviest. None of this is damage. A scuff on full-grain honey leather is usually buffed out within days by the same oils and friction that are darkening it — the mark doesn't stay, but the effect of it does, compounding a little more each week. A year of genuine daily carry reads as a noticeably richer, more amber tone and a noticeably softer hand than the bag had on day one. That's the patina doing its job, and it's the reason the bag is built to be used hard rather than kept precious.

## Caring for it day to day

None of this asks for much, which is part of the point.

**Let it get wet, then let it dry slowly.** Full-grain honey leather can take a rain shower on the way to work. What it can't take is a radiator or direct heat while still damp — that pulls the oils out fast and leaves the hide stiff. Wipe off standing water, let it sit at room temperature away from direct heat, and it dries out fine on its own.

**Condition it lightly, twice a year.** A plain leather conditioner, worked in with a soft cloth and buffed off after a few minutes, keeps the hide supple as it darkens. More often than that just leaves it oversaturated. You're feeding it, not coating it.

**Leave the marks alone.** Don't reach for a cleaner every time a scuff shows up — on full-grain leather, most of them fade on their own within a week under ordinary handling. Trying to erase every mark just works against the patina you're actually after.

**Use it instead of storing it.** A tote left in a closet for months stiffens in whatever shape it's left in. Daily carry is what keeps a vegetable-tanned bag supple — regular use is the maintenance routine, not a departure from it.
